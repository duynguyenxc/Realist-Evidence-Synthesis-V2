2. Programme theory (system-generated, v10)

Sign-off: PENDING (HITL-4)

Across the evidence pack, educational interventions develop analytical and non-analytical clinical reasoning less by teaching a single superior reasoning mode than by creating a fit between student context, case demands, and instructional design. Productive pathways recur when case-based practice, contrastive examples, schemas, prompts, model explanations, and feedback make diagnostic relations visible and usable. These resources trigger Structured Diagnostic Knowledge Building, Guided Self-Explanation, Structured reasoning uptake, and sometimes Pressure-driven case patterning, leading to improved diagnostic accuracy, reasoning-process development, knowledge retention, or Error-Resistant Diagnostic Learning. Negative pathways recur when students have insufficient organised prior knowledge, the case is too complex, the strategy is abstract or time-consuming, feedback is thin, or practice is unguided; then the same broad resources may trigger Unguided Diagnostic Reasoning, confusion, overload, superficial feature use, or non-use.

Context 1: Novices with limited or poorly organised diagnostic knowledge who receive bounded, concrete scaffolding

*(C) When students are novices or have deficient, poorly organised illness-script knowledge, and the diagnostic task is bounded enough for them to use support, (Mresource) Contrastive Diagnostic Scaffolding, diagnostic schemas, worked examples with elaborated feedback, explicit combined analytic/non-analytic instructions, and stepwise Reasoning Practice Scaffolding make relevant features, alternatives, errors, and expert reasoning visible; (Mresponse) learners engage in Structured Diagnostic Knowledge Building, Guided Self-Explanation, feature comparison, and Structured reasoning uptake rather than relying on unguided first impressions; (O) diagnostic accuracy, retained structured knowledge, reasoning-process development, and Error-Resistant Diagnostic Learning improve.*

For low-expertise learners, the generative mechanism is not the mere presence of cases or strategy instruction. The resource must organise the task so that students can notice diagnostically decisive contrasts and connect features to categories. When that happens, learners build or refine illness-script structures and can combine analytic checking with similarity-based recognition. When the resource is abstract, under-explained, or too broad, learners may perform Unguided Diagnostic Reasoning, over-focus on isolated features, or become confused, producing no gain or poorer performance.

Supporting studies: S004, S005, S006, S016, S017, S019, S021, S024, S028

Conflicting evidence: The evidence is not a simple ‘novices need more instruction’ pattern. Concrete, case-linked and bounded supports helped novices, as in combined ECG reasoning instruction, schema-based murmur learning, analytic or non-analytic cytopathology training, structured reflection, and worked examples with elaborated feedback. However, similarly labelled supports backfired or failed when they increased complexity without usable guidance: diagnosis-specific tables could promote incorrect feature identification in an unknown murmur, combined cytopathology strategy instruction could confuse learners at final test, erroneous examples with right/wrong feedback alone left learners superficial, and cognitive-forcing or bias seminars did not reliably reduce error. Thus the contradiction is explained by whether the resource reduced the novice’s search space and made diagnostic relations usable, or instead added an extra reasoning demand before sufficient knowledge organisation existed.

Context 2: Partially prepared learners with incomplete or error-prone prior knowledge

*(C) When students have some prior clinical or domain exposure but their knowledge remains incomplete, error-prone, or insufficiently integrated, (Mresource) repeated varied case exposure, same-process cases, self-explanation tasks, model comparison, key-feature testing, and feedback/corrective guidance provide opportunities to retrieve, compare, explain, and correct diagnostic reasoning; (Mresponse) learners engage in Guided Self-Explanation, Structured Diagnostic Knowledge Building, hypothesis testing, error debugging, and, with repeated exposure, Pressure-driven case patterning; (O) they develop stronger case representations, better knowledge integration, improved diagnostic performance, and longer-term retention.*

For learners beyond absolute novice status, case work becomes more powerful when it prompts them to retrieve and test what they already know. Varied cases and feedback help them compare enabling conditions, consequences, and pathophysiological links. Self-explanation and model comparison can reactivate biomedical mechanisms and support knowledge restructuring. However, these mechanisms depend on a threshold of usable prior knowledge and on feedback or prompts that keep learners from merely rehearsing misconceptions.

Supporting studies: S013, S018, S020, S022, S025, S026, S027

Conflicting evidence: Self-explanation, repeated cases, and feedback produced both positive and limited effects. The realist resolution is that they worked when students had enough prior knowledge to generate explanations or compare cases but still had gaps that could be repaired. They were weaker when prior knowledge was so poor that students could not detect or solve errors, when feedback was reduced, or when students were asked to diagnose and explain cases without a suggested learning strategy. In this context, productive difficulty can stimulate biomedical debugging; unsupported difficulty leaves errors unrecognised.

Context 3: Unfamiliar clinical problems with room for illness-script elaboration

*(C) When students work in unfamiliar or less practised clinical domains where their illness scripts are not yet well developed, (Mresource) self-explanation, reflection prompts, case texts, and cognitively congruent expert or resident self-explanation models provide occasions to link symptoms, mechanisms, and diagnostic categories; (Mresponse) learners use Guided Self-Explanation to generate inferences, monitor knowledge limits, reactivate biomedical explanations, and restructure representations; (O) diagnostic accuracy and knowledge-and-expertise building improve, especially for unfamiliar or near/far-transfer cases when prompts are embedded.*

This context refines the prior-knowledge hypothesis: benefit is greatest not simply for ‘low’ or ‘high’ knowledge, but where students possess enough relevant knowledge to elaborate yet still lack stable scripts. Guided Self-Explanation is the key mechanism response. It converts unfamiliarity into active sense-making, causal linking, and monitoring of uncertainty. Without that room or prompt for elaboration, the intervention can become redundant or passive.

Supporting studies: S013, S020, S025, S026

Conflicting evidence: The same self-explanation family did not reliably help familiar cases or already well-defined illness scripts. In familiar domains, students may have little need or room for reconstruction and may generate fewer biomedical inferences, producing no added diagnostic benefit. In difficult far-transfer cases, unprompted resident examples were insufficient because silent observation did not ensure active processing. The positive pathway appears when unfamiliarity creates a productive gap and the resource prompts active explanation or comparison; the negative pathway appears when familiarity reduces elaborative need or when difficulty is not accompanied by processing prompts.

Context 4: Confusable or ambiguous cases at a manageable level of complexity

*(C) When cases are confusable, ambiguous, or contain misleading features, but the complexity is matched to students’ knowledge, (Mresource) Contrastive Diagnostic Scaffolding, compare-and-contrast learning, evidence tables, feature-focused scaffolds, and reasoning prompts/reflection direct attention to similarities, differences, supporting findings, opposing findings, and absent findings; (Mresponse) learners develop diagnosticity awareness, check premature closure, compare alternatives, and combine analytic feature checking with non-analytic familiarity judgements; (O) diagnostic accuracy improves and learners become more resistant to misleading information or miss errors.*

Ambiguous cases can stimulate both analytical and non-analytical development. Comparison builds similarity-based pattern recognition while evidence prompts discipline analytic checking. The outcome is not guaranteed by ambiguity alone: the case must be sufficiently structured for students to identify which features matter and why. Otherwise, students may attend to irrelevant differences, search for nonexistent diagnoses, or experience cognitive overload.

Supporting studies: S004, S005, S016, S019, S020, S021, S024, S025, S026

Conflicting evidence: Contrast, reflection, and feature-focus were beneficial when they highlighted decisive differences among confusable diagnoses. Yet the same families produced limited benefit or negative effects when cases were too complex relative to learners’ knowledge or when prompts encouraged unhelpful over-searching. Differential diagnosis evidence tables may trigger consideration of alternatives, but if students lack the medical knowledge to evaluate those alternatives, the reasoning may not help. Cognitive-forcing strategies may add circumspection demands without reducing error. Thus ambiguity is productive only when scaffolds make the relevant contrasts tractable.

Context 5: Limited clinical exposure, short placements, or restricted access to longitudinal patient experience

*(C) When students have limited real patient exposure, short rotations, or few opportunities to follow illness trajectories, (Mresource) simulation, virtual patients, EHR-based casebooks, video-enriched cases, temporally organised records, physician reasoning annotations, and key-feature case testing provide multiplied, standardised, longitudinal, or otherwise unavailable case encounters; (Mresponse) learners show Structured reasoning uptake, practise and refine reasoning, observe test-of-time and management effects, build pattern recognition across cases, and access physician thought processes; (O) engagement, perceived value, illness-script development, clinical reasoning skill acquisition, and procedural knowledge retention improve.*

In this context, the educational resource compensates for scarcity of authentic encounters. Video, virtual, and EHR cases create repeated exposure to presentations, management decisions, and consequences that students might not otherwise see. Annotations and key-feature prompts make the reasoning process visible. The learner response is uptake of structured reasoning opportunities and accumulation of case patterns, supporting both explicit decision-making and non-analytical recognition.

Supporting studies: S011, S015, S022, S023, S027, S028

Conflicting evidence: Simulation and virtual or recorded cases were not uniformly sufficient. They were valued when they substituted for missing exposure, compressed longitudinal experience, or made real clinician reasoning visible. They were weaker or negative when the learning resource was perceived as irrelevant, theoretical, unfamiliar, or too complex for immediate practice. For example, a clinical decision-making theory tutorial did not change described approaches, and unfamiliar mnemonics were not used when seen as time-consuming. The contradiction is explained by whether the resource solved an exposure problem in a usable way or merely added abstract content/tool burden.

Context 6: Pressure and task demand that are challenging but containable

*(C) When students face time pressure, competing tasks, acute deterioration, or game-like challenge, and have enough prior experience or familiar structure to cope, (Mresource) repeated time-limited simulated cases, competitive quiz formats, solution-focused feedback, and familiar communication structures provide pressured but bounded practice; (Mresponse) learners engage in Pressure-driven case patterning, enjoyment/challenge, rapid hypothesis testing, readjustment, and Structured reasoning uptake; (O) engagement, voluntary practice, faster or improved case-solving performance, and practical reasoning development may occur.*

This section refines the cognitive-load element of the programme theory. Time pressure can accelerate non-analytical patterning and motivate practice, but only if students can keep the task within a manageable cognitive envelope. Familiar tools such as SBAR can reduce fear and support action, whereas unfamiliar or complex tools can be rejected. Productive pressure supports rapid case representation; excessive pressure disrupts structured assessment.

Supporting studies: S015, S022

Conflicting evidence: Pressure was double-edged. It promoted enjoyment, voluntary practice, hypothesis testing, and performance gains when repeated time-limited cases were paired with feedback and when learners had relevant experience or familiar structures. It produced frustration, stress, overload, loss of focus, and incomplete assessment when students lacked prioritisation training, were unfamiliar with pagers or acute multitasking, or had limited clinical experience under very short time limits. Thus pressure is not a mechanism by itself; it becomes productive only when bounded by experience, feedback, and usable routines.

Context 7: Opaque expert reasoning and need for reasoning visibility

*(C) When students are expected to learn expert reasoning from clinical encounters but expert cognition is tacit, automated, or opaque, (Mresource) explicit modelling, physician reasoning annotations, teacher-led consultation pauses, hypothesis-listing prompts, reasoning-probe questions, and annotated real cases make expert reasoning inspectable; (Mresponse) learners engage in Structured reasoning uptake, active tracking of demonstrated reasoning, comparison with their own ideas, and understanding of physician thought processes; (O) they develop clinical reasoning ability, decision-making skills, and capacity to generate primary and differential hypotheses.*

The key resource is not expert presence alone but visible reasoning. Students need access to the hypotheses, discriminating cues, uncertainty management, and causal links that experts use. When modelling is annotated or interrupted with reasoning probes, learners can actively track and compare reasoning. When the model is tacit, erroneous, or unprompted, learners may remain passive or fail to extract transferable reasoning principles.

Supporting studies: S023, S025, S026, S028

Conflicting evidence: Modelling helped when it was concrete, case-linked, and cognitively accessible. Tacit exposure to automated clinician reasoning was a barrier because novices could not infer the reasoning process. Same-level peer models had limited added value when they contained irrelevant details or uncorrected errors. Unprompted resident examples were less useful for difficult transfer because observation alone did not ensure active processing. The difference lies in whether the model makes reasoning visible and usable, and whether prompts require learners to process it actively.
